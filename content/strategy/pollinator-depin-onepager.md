## The problem

People say beekeeping is bad for wild bees. New research says they are
right. On the island of Giannutri, closing the hives for part of the day
gave wild bees about 60% more nectar and 30% more pollen. In France, dense
beekeeping cut wild bee presence by 55% up to 1 km from the apiary.

Nobody measures this. Bee hotels are sold everywhere, but a study of 600 of
them found more wasps than bees inside, and no data that they help at all.

We are beekeepers. So we want to measure it, not argue about it.

## What we build

A small house for wild bees (mason bees) with sensors inside and a weather
sensor outside. Same system we use in our hives, in a smaller box.

Inside: temperature, humidity, a counter at the entrance, a camera that
takes a photo of the nesting tubes every day. Outside: temperature,
humidity, pressure, light.

The first houses go next to our own hives on the farm. Then to people and
companies who want one. All data is public. Every house is a device in the
HiveBits network on Solana, with its own on-chain identity, like our hives.

## What a host gets

- The house with sensors and the first bees (cocoons), so year one is not empty.
- The house in the app: live data, daily photo, "first bee moved in" messages.
- $NECTAR for keeping the house online and sending data.
- A yearly service: send the nesting block back, get it cleaned and refilled.
- Companies also get a biodiversity report for ESG and a live page for employees.

## Who pays

- Companies that adopt houses for ESG. This works today: 3Bee in Italy sells
  it to 500+ brands.
- Food brands and growers that need proof their supply chain is pollinator
  safe. M&S and Importaco already pay for pollinator sensors on farms.
- Later: certification and EU nature credits, which will need proof that is
  not collected by hand.

Raw weather data alone has no buyer, WeatherXM showed that. Pollinator
activity with weather next to it, for a specific place, does.
